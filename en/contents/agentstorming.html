uEngine AX Consulting · Workshop

Agent Storming

A workshop for designing how people and AI work together. The people who do the work uncover how it really flows, then design, run and improve processes where people and AI agents collaborate.

Download the guide (PPTX, Korean) View PDF (Korean) Where to start

Background

Agents succeed or fail on the boundaries of the work, not on the model

When companies put AI agents to work, the obstacles come less from the model’s reasoning than from a lack of understanding of the work. Without domain context, a model gives answers that look reasonable while breaking real business rules. With several agents, responsibility blurs, data leaks between contexts and the agents become entangled. The ‘Big Ball of Mud’ that software engineering has long warned about reappears at the agent layer.

That is why architects in academia and industry have begun using domain-driven design (DDD) and EventStorming to find and design agents. AWS divided flight operations and the customer journey by bounded context to derive dedicated agents, and consultancies such as Orium use EventStorming to diagnose manual bottlenecks and set adoption priorities and roadmaps.

The value of EventStorming is that it shows a company’s reality as it is. Manual workarounds, system gaps and informal collaboration that appear in no policy document come up on the wall, and the hotspots marked there become agent opportunities. Domain events lead to triggers, commands to tool calls, policies to guardrails and read models to RAG context.

Agent Storming is uEngine’s workshop approach for carrying this out in the field, organized into workshop steps, decision criteria, role assignments and an adoption roadmap.

Why Agent Storming

Agent adoption fails in two places at once

Enterprise AI doesn’t succeed or fail on the size of the model. It depends on how clearly you hand agents the boundaries and responsibilities of the work.

76.5%

Share of all failures that arise in design and collaboration structure

UC Berkeley MAST

Failure in choosing what to automate

Where to use agents

Choosing targets around the technology drives up cost, blurs the business value and weakens risk control. Many use cases marketed as agentic don’t need an agent at all (Gartner).

Failure in designing the units

How to divide them

Dividing by technology or org chart loses the meaning of the work. Merge everything and you overload context; split too finely and you get broken context and conflicting decisions. Adding or removing agents alone doesn’t fix it.

Gartner’s advice — Rather than bolting agents onto existing work, redesign the workflow from the ground up. That is the path to success.

Three Design Outputs

One EventStorming session finds the place, the boundary and the design material

Three design outputs that move past the limits of a technology-first approach.

Place · Where to use them?

Where agents belong

Turn hotspots where observing, deciding and acting are possible into agent opportunities.

Failures it prevents: targets with unclear value, use cases chosen by trend

Responsibility · How far to trust them?

The agent’s boundary

Assign a bounded context with consistent terms and rules as the area of responsibility.

Failures it prevents: context overload, conflicting decisions, blurred responsibility

Material · What drives them?

The agent’s design material

Carry the nine artifacts from the workshop into the system architecture and code.

Failures it prevents: broken business rules, hallucinations from conflicting policies

Workshop

A five-step workshop lays out the work and draws the boundaries

Lay out the work as events in time order, draw boundaries and specify agent candidates. We recommend 8 to 12 people who know one work journey together.

1

Lay out events

Arrange what happened in the work, as past-tense events, in time order.

2

Mark pivotal events

Mark points of no return in bold to set reference points.

3

Draw boundaries

Divide contexts by pivotal events, changes in terms and changes in ownership.

4

Mark hotspots

Find bottlenecks and unclear spots and mark them with a red diamond.

5

Opportunity and spec

Pick candidates per context and choose the first pilot most likely to succeed.

From Stickies to Agents

The stickies on the wall become an agent’s design material

How the nine artifacts of an EventStorming workshop translate into the parts of an AI agent.

StickyMeaning in the domainRole in an AI agent
Domain EventorangeA fact that has already happenedAsynchronous trigger input; task-completion output message
CommandblueA command and intent that cause a state changeAgent task instructions; tool-call (Function Calling) signals and actions
PolicylilacA business rule that reacts to conditionsSystem prompt, conditional routing and guardrails (rules that stop execution outside the allowed scope)
Read ModelgreenAn information view for decision-makingRAG context, in-memory view, data lookups for judgment and context
AggregateyellowThe smallest unit of transactional consistencyPersistent state store; scope of write permissions. Also, as data, an ontology or a knowledge graph, the smallest unit that can serve as the body of an agent
ActoryellowWhoever performs the workAn AI agent or a person (human-in-the-loop)
External SystempinkThird-party and legacy services outside the domainExternal SaaS subscriptions; legacy core-system APIs
HotspotredFriction, delays, bottlenecks and unresolved risksPrime candidates for automation and innovation
Bounded Contextdashed boundaryThe boundary within which terms and models holdA single agent’s area of responsibility, or a multi-agent team’s priorities and boundaries
The yellow sticky can be the body of an agent — An aggregate can be data, or it can be an ontology or a knowledge graph. Because this is where it is settled what an agent knows and how far it can change things, uEngine sees the aggregate as one of the things that can serve as the body of an agent. If the bounded context is the agent’s boundary of responsibility, the aggregate can be the agent’s smallest unit within it.

Validate the Opportunity

Not every hotspot should become an agent

For each hotspot, O.D.A tests whether an agent can observe, decide and act on its own. If any answer is no, consider rule-based automation or leaving it with people.

O · Observe

Observe

Can it receive the events and data it needs?

Domain events, read models

D · Decide

Decide

Can it set a course using policy and context?

Policies, RAG context

A · Act

Act

Can it carry out the work through tool calls?

Commands, external system APIs

If all three are “yes”

Reclassify it as an agent opportunity and rank it as a first-pilot candidate.

Choose who does the work by its nature

CategoryRule automation and RPAIntelligent AI agentsPeople
Decision typeDeterministic (same input, same result)Contextual, adaptiveStrategic, political judgment
Form of dataStructured dataUnstructured dataTacit knowledge, complex stakeholder interests
Tolerance for failure riskVery lowMedium (offset by guardrails and human intervention)Very low (legal and ethical accountability concentrates here)
Typical workAccount provisioning, simple data migration, fixed-amount approvals on structured dataAssisting unstructured claims review, predicting supply-chain bottlenecks, rebooking complex journeysFinal approval of large credit reviews, reorganizations, strategy for new domains
First pilot — Start with the task where the felt impact on the front line is highest while risk stays tightly controlled (High Impact / Low Risk).

Boundary

Decide where the boundary goes, not how many agents you have

A good unit emerges when five conditions line up inside one boundary. A bounded context is a design hypothesis that ties them together, and domains and agents are not fixed one-to-one. Keep testing and adjusting against authority, state, cost, risk and responsibility.

ConditionDefinitionDDD elementProblem it preventsWhere EventStorming finds it
1. Consistent languageTerms in prompts, tool descriptions and data share one meaningUbiquitous languageContext overload, confused termsWhere terms change
2. Owned stateThe data an agent may change is clearly scopedAggregateConflicting decisionsAggregate
3. Explicit rulesThe business rules to follow sit together inside the boundaryPolicies, invariantsBroken business rulesPolicy
4. Narrow interfaceExchanges with other units happen only through agreed eventsDomain eventBroken context, tangled dependenciesDomain event
5. Clear responsibilityThe owning team and human approval points are definedTeam that owns the contextBlurred responsibilityActor

Four signals for drawing a boundary

Where a term’s meaning changes

Is the same word used to mean different things?

Pivotal events

Is this a point of no return?

Where ownership changes

Does the work pass to another team?

How far state changes together

Is this data that must stay consistent in one step?

Where several signals overlap is the strongest boundary candidate, and domain experts always make the final call. Data areas with different security or regulatory classifications become separate contexts regardless of other signals (per Microsoft).

Start with a single agent — Vendor guidance from Microsoft and OpenAI agrees: start with one agent and split only when there is a clear boundary.

The limits of autonomy, and final responsibility with people

People

Handle value judgments and exceptions. Hold the highest authority and final approval.

Agents

Handle pattern analysis, drafting and repetitive work. Limited autonomy, operating under human approval conditions.

External systems

Handle record keeping and tool execution. Run automatically after final human approval.

Five lanes (requester, agent, owner, approver, system) define who does what and when. AI helps work move quickly and efficiently, but exceptions and final decisions return to people by designed rules.

Validate small with Process GPT — Instead of a big-bang rollout, simulate the designed BPMN model in Process GPT at the smallest possible unit to verify what is actually supported. The Process GPT features and screens in the guide are proposal-stage hypotheses before real validation, not a specification of current features.

Roadmap

A five-stage roadmap for company-wide AI agent adoption

Don’t convert everything at once. Release a single pilot to a limited group first, adjust through four-week evaluations and expand carefully into adjacent contexts.

StageCore activitiesKey deliverables
1. AlignBig-picture EventStorming, surfacing business events, identifying context boundariesIntegrated timeline board, draft contexts, ubiquitous language glossary
2. PrioritizeTurning hotspots into agent opportunities, assessing impact and risk, selecting a pilotAgent opportunity backlog, single-pilot definition, key performance indicators (KPIs)
3. DesignDefining event schemas and state models, defining approval guardrailsArchitecture on a Page, OpenAPI spec, guardrail spec
4. BuildBuilding the RAG retrieval base, orchestration and tool-call integration; release to limited usersWorkflow code, monitoring dashboard, performance evaluation suite
5. Iterate & ScaleFour-week evaluations of operating data, tuning prompts and guardrails, expanding to adjacent contextsAgent performance report, context map diagram, governance guidelines

Three final deliverables for executives

Multi-agent collaboration map

Specifies goals, responsibilities and authority for people and agents.

Executable BPMN process

Orchestration including approval steps and exception-handling gateways.

Results and experiment metrics (KPIs)

Tracks the pre-adoption baseline, quality and cost, and the human intervention rate.

Every deliverable, from event discovery through opportunity finding and authority design to final execution metrics, stays traceable end to end.

Limits of adoption and responses

AreaLimit or riskResponse
Cold startPsychological resistance to a blank page; AI anchoring bias that misses exceptionsUse AI drafts only as a starting point, and always fill in exceptions with domain experts.
Big-bang attemptsAutomating everything brings unpredictability and a surge in cost and debugging difficultyStart with a lightweight pilot on one hotspot, evaluate every four weeks, then expand.
Domain judgmentTools or AI can’t decide real ownership boundaries and standard terms on their ownDomain experts set bounded context boundaries and terms themselves.
High-risk zonesWithout human control: regulatory violations and legal liabilityHard-code human-in-the-loop approval guardrails into the context spec.
