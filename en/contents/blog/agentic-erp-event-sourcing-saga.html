Rethinking ERP Architecture for Long-Running Agentic AI: Why Event Sourcing and Saga Rollback?

Date: November 04, 2026

  1. When AI does the work, there is work to undo

Businesses are asking AI to do more. Beyond answering questions, agents enter company systems to place orders and change delivery assignments. They must be accountable for the work they change as well as the answers they give.

This work takes more than a few seconds. Finding a material shortage, choosing another supplier, requesting purchase approval, receiving goods, and changing the production schedule can take days. This is long-running work.

Along the way, an AI mistake or a supplier stockout may require completed work to be undone. A manager may reject approval. Other employees keep working too. Reporting a failure does not clear the materials already reserved or the purchase orders already created.

Here is an example. There are 1,000 units of a part in stock. At 9 a.m. on Monday, an agent misreads demand and reserves 200 units. At 11 a.m. on Tuesday, warehouse staff ship 30 units for another order. At 2 p.m. on Tuesday, a manager spots the mistaken reservation and rejects approval. Now only the agent's work must be undone. The 30 units shipped by the employee must stay shipped, leaving 970 units in stock.

The key question is simple.

How can we safely undo work already done?

  2. MCP standardizes connections, but does not define undo

Previously, connecting AI meant writing integration code for each system. MCP is a shared protocol for connecting AI to external tools: a standard port. That is why its official documentation compares it to USB-C. A shared connection lets agents call tools under common rules.

Work follows three steps.

  1. Read: Check parts inventory and recent purchase prices.
  2. Decide: Weigh goals and limits, then choose tasks and their order.
  3. Act: Reserve materials and create purchase orders.

The challenge is acting. If approval is denied after materials are reserved and a purchase order is created, that work needs to be undone. The MCP tools specification dated 2025-06-18 does not define undo. This reflects a division of responsibilities, not a flaw in MCP. The business system behind the tools—the ERP—must handle it.

  3. How is the same work recorded in state-based and event-sourced systems?

How can the ERP recover from the mistake above? The answer depends on how it stores records.

A football scoreboard showing 2-1 tells you the score and nothing else. With the match record you can rebuild the scoreboard at any time, but the scoreboard alone cannot bring back the match record. A state-based system is the scoreboard; an event-sourced system is the match record. See how the mistake above is recorded in each system.

State-based systems can also address much of this by adding history tables or a data warehouse. The difference is whether those records are the source of truth or an addition.

To undo a mistake, you need to know what was done, what the values were at the time, and which actions belonged to other people. With event sourcing, this information is in the records themselves.

  4. Three problems with state-based ERP

A traditional ERP is the main business ledger, or system of record (SoR). It overwrites table values with the latest values. This is the state-based approach described in the table above. This was familiar and intuitive when people clicked Save on screen. But keeping only current values creates problems when an agent's work needs to be cancelled days later.

The reason disappears

In the example above, when inventory changes from 1,000 → 800, the current value only shows 800. Without knowing what reduced it by 200 units, it is hard to know what to cancel. Restoring numbers without that context can leave records out of step with actual work (State Drift).

Time gaps cause trouble

In the example above, materials were reserved at 9 a.m. on Monday, then approval was denied at 2 p.m. on Tuesday. A database ROLLBACK on Tuesday cannot cancel work already committed on Monday. Overwriting the old value can also erase work done by other employees in between. This is called a lost update.

Erasing records causes audit problems

Accounting ledgers and manufacturing histories must not be quietly erased. Records must show who changed what, when, and why it was cancelled. The US FDA's 21 CFR 11.10(e) also requires changes to covered records not to obscure earlier records. It does not prescribe a storage method.

Separate history tables can help, but these extra records may be missing or differ from the original data.

  5. Use audit records as the source, and add cancelling actions instead of erasing

Event sourcing: a ledger that keeps every entry

Record +KRW 100,000, -KRW 30,000, and +KRW 50,000 in a bank account, and the balance is their sum. The five rows above are like that transaction history. Each row is called an event.

Most systems treat current values as the source and keep audit records separately. Event sourcing reverses this: the audit records themselves are the source, and current values are calculated from them. Audit data is the basis for calculating inventory on screen and finding work to cancel.

An agent's work builds up in the same way. Event names use the past tense because they describe things that have already happened.

StockReservedByAgent: Reserve 200 parts
PurchaseOrderCreated: Create a purchase order
ManagerApprovalRejected: Manager rejects approval

As Pat Helland put it in “Immutability Changes Everything” (2015), “Accountants don't use erasers.” Keep the mistake and add a record of its correction.

Sagas: add cancelling actions instead of erasing the past

A saga groups a task's steps together. If work stops partway through, it cancels completed steps in reverse order. A cancelling action that offsets the original action is called a compensating transaction. The concept comes from Garcia-Molina and Salem's paper “Sagas” (1987).

Cancelling a flight booking does not restore an old seat count. It adds a new action that cancels the reservation. This leaves other bookings made in the meantime intact. In a factory, if a process using issued materials is cancelled, the materials are received back into stock instead of erasing the issue record.

Having records does not mean every action can be cancelled. The system must check for later work that depends on it and leave cases it cannot cancel for a person to handle.

  6. Why use both together?

“Could we just add sagas to an existing ERP?” Yes. Sagas were originally designed for ordinary databases. But reliable undo needs two things.

  • Know the original values. If 100 units were reserved at KRW 1,250 after exchange rates and discounts, cancellation needs those terms and quantities, not today's price. The business records, called events, hold those values. With only current values, you must find them in separate history records.
  • Stay safe when a request arrives twice. Repeating the same request must give the same result (idempotency). If a response is lost and cancellation is requested twice, cancelling 100 units could turn into cancelling 200. Event versions, saga IDs, and request IDs filter out the second request. Event sourcing does not do this automatically; these checks must be built too.

A fixed workflow has steps and cancellation rules designed in advance. Agents choose tools as circumstances change. Even with cancellation rules for each tool, the system must read the completed work's records to decide what to undo. A state-based ERP needs to collect those records separately, effectively creating another ledger.

  7. Test outcomes first with the same ledger

Because nothing was erased, past records are still available. Inventory records can show whether weak dehumidifier sales last summer came from low demand or stockouts. Sales totals alone cannot tell you that.

What-if analysis calculates the original plan and a changed plan side by side. It adds nothing to the ledger. The numbers come from the same models used on the ERP screen, not from the LLM. People can ask and agents can answer. Agents do not yet run these calculations and decide on their own.

Across 60 virtual companies, the median quarterly revenue error was 6.6%. These were virtual tests; results at real companies may be worse. The predicted ranges for a one-year outlook were too narrow, so the system warns when looking more than 180 days ahead.

Test first, act, and undo mistakes. All three use the same ledger.

  8. Use the same ledger for faster dashboards and to find how work gets done

Separating writes from reads (CQRS) works well with event sourcing. Commands write events to the ledger. The read side uses those events to prepare the tables it needs. uEngine ERP separates figures that must be exact from those that can lag by a few seconds.

  • Figures that must be exact, as in closing the books. Inventory and journal entries are also written to separate tables within the same transaction as the command. Even with three years of data from a virtual company, the trial balance takes 0.016 seconds and the balance sheet takes 0.011 seconds.
  • Dashboards and statistics. The read model takes in new events every 5 seconds and updates only what has changed. Order confirmation does not wait for the read model, and the dashboard catches up within a few seconds.

Because events are the source records, the read model can be discarded and rebuilt at any time. A report designed today can include all three years of history. With only current values, state-based data makes it hard to fill in past periods for a new report. These timings used virtual company data on a development PC running other tasks too. Focus on the size of the difference between the two methods rather than the absolute times.

The ledger is already a process mining log

Process mining uses records to find the actual sequence of steps taken to handle an order. With state-based data, change history tables must be extracted separately to piece together what happened. The event sourcing ledger already holds those records.

uEngine ERP groups ledger records into order-to-cash and procure-to-pay cases and exports them as CSV, XES, and OCEL 2.0 files. Uploading these files to Process GPT produces BPMN without a language model. Three years of orders from a virtual company—3,287 orders—became a diagram in under 0.1 seconds, and every case could follow the paths in that diagram (100% replay fitness). The resulting process becomes a business process definition after review and approval by the person responsible.

Undo, test outcomes ahead of time, build fast dashboards, and find how work gets done. All use the same ledger.

  10. Success depends on a system that can handle mistakes

It is easy to focus only on the latest LLM and prompts. Once an agent operates ERP, the key question changes: “Can our system safely handle AI's mistakes?”

Not every system needs event sourcing. Microsoft's Event Sourcing pattern document also explains that traditional methods are enough for most systems. Start with systems where agents change transactions, inventory, and payments. Ask four questions before adopting them.

  • Can we ask what will change before acting?
  • Can we explain what the agent did?
  • Can we undo it without disturbing someone else's work?
  • If undo is not possible, can it stop and hand over to the person responsible?

Success depends on a system that can handle mistakes, not the model's intelligence.
