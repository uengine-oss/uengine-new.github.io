Rethinking ERP Architecture for Long-Running Agentic AI: Why Event Sourcing and Saga Rollback?

Date: November 04, 2026

  At a glance: state-based and event-sourced systems

What a business system stores as its source of truth changes what AI can do when it works.

ComparisonState-based system (overwrites current values)Event-sourced system (keeps every event)
What it storesOnly the current value is the source of truth, such as 800 units in stock.Every event is the source of truth, such as stock falling by 200 units from 1,000. Current values are calculated from those records.
UndoOverwriting with earlier values may lose work others have done in between.Adds cancellation records to offset only its own work.
Audit and traceabilityRelies on separate logs, which may be missing or differ from the original data.The records are the source of truth, keeping who did what, when, and why.
Past analysisFind the past state in backups or separate history records.Recalculates the state at a chosen point from the records. Distinguishes whether low sales last summer came from low demand or stockouts.
ForecastingForecasts from sales records. Demand lost to stockouts can look low, so estimates are used to adjust for it.Checks inventory records for stockouts and accounts for them. Runs the same forecast on the ledger from a year earlier and compares it with actual results.
Testing before acting (what-if)Must first gather the history needed to calculate results under changed conditions.Calculates the original plan and a changed plan side by side using accumulated records. Nothing is added to the ledger.
Agent actionsFind what the agent did in conversations or separate records.Assigns an ID to the work, reads its scope, and stops when it cannot proceed.
CostSimple and familiar. Sufficient for most systems.Needs more storage and has short delays between modules. Moving an existing system is also costly.

State-based systems can also address much of this by adding history tables or a data warehouse. The difference is whether those records are the source of truth or an addition.

  1. From AI that answers to AI that acts

Businesses are asking AI to do more. Beyond answering questions, agents enter company systems to place orders and change delivery assignments. They must be accountable for the work they change as well as the answers they give.

This work takes more than a few seconds. Finding a material shortage, choosing another supplier, requesting purchase approval, receiving goods, and changing the production schedule can take days. This is long-running work.

Along the way, AI may make a bad decision or a supplier may run out of stock. A manager may reject approval. Other employees keep working too. Reporting a failure does not clear the materials already reserved or the purchase orders already created. The key question is simple.

How can we safely undo work already done?

  2. MCP standardizes connections, but does not define undo

Previously, connecting AI meant writing integration code for each system. MCP is a shared protocol for connecting AI to external tools: a standard port. That is why its official documentation compares it to USB-C. A shared connection lets agents call tools under common rules.

Work follows three steps.

  1. Read: Check parts inventory and recent purchase prices.
  2. Decide: Weigh goals and limits, then choose tasks and their order.
  3. Act: Reserve materials and create purchase orders.

The challenge is acting. If approval is denied after materials are reserved and a purchase order is created, that work needs to be undone. The MCP tools specification dated 2025-06-18 does not define undo. This reflects a division of responsibilities, not a flaw in MCP. The business system behind the tools—the ERP—must handle it.

  3. Three problems when ERP overwrites values

A traditional ERP is the main business ledger, or system of record (SoR). It overwrites table values with the latest values. This is the state-based approach described in the table above. This was familiar and intuitive when people clicked Save on screen. But keeping only current values creates problems when an agent's work needs to be cancelled days later.

The reason disappears

When inventory changes from 1,000 → 800, the current value only shows 800. Without knowing what reduced it by 200 units, it is hard to know what to cancel. Restoring numbers without that context can leave records out of step with actual work (State Drift).

Time gaps cause trouble

Materials may be reserved at 9 a.m. on Monday, then approval denied on Tuesday afternoon. A database ROLLBACK on Tuesday cannot cancel work already committed on Monday. Overwriting the old value can also erase work done by other employees in between. This is called a lost update.

Erasing records causes audit problems

Accounting ledgers and manufacturing histories must not be quietly erased. Records must show who changed what, when, and why it was cancelled. The US FDA's 21 CFR 11.10(e) also requires changes to covered records not to obscure earlier records. It does not prescribe a storage method.

Separate history tables can help, but these extra records may be missing or differ from the original data.

  4. Use audit records as the source, and add cancelling actions instead of erasing

Event sourcing: a ledger that keeps every entry

Think of a bank account. Record +KRW 100,000, -KRW 30,000, and +KRW 50,000 in order, then add them up to find the balance. The transaction history shows deposits and withdrawals that the final balance alone cannot explain. Recording what happened in order and calculating current values from it is called event sourcing.

Most systems treat current values as the source and keep audit records separately. Event sourcing reverses this: the audit records themselves are the source, and current values are calculated from them. Audit data is more than supporting material for a later inspection. It is the basis for calculating inventory on screen and finding work to cancel.

An agent's work builds up like this. Each entry keeps the items and conditions involved at the time.

StockReservedByAgent: Reserve 200 parts
PurchaseOrderCreated: Create a purchase order
ManagerApprovalRejected: Manager rejects approval

As Pat Helland put it in “Immutability Changes Everything” (2015), “Accountants don't use erasers.” Keep the mistake and add a record of its correction.

Sagas: add cancelling actions instead of erasing the past

A saga groups a task's steps together. If work stops partway through, it cancels completed steps in reverse order. A cancelling action that offsets the original action is called a compensating transaction. The concept comes from Garcia-Molina and Salem's paper “Sagas” (1987).

Cancelling a flight booking does not restore an old seat count. It adds a new action that cancels the reservation. This leaves other bookings made in the meantime intact. In a factory, if a process using issued materials is cancelled, the materials are received back into stock instead of erasing the issue record.

Having records does not mean every action can be cancelled. The system must check for later work that depends on it and leave cases it cannot cancel for a person to handle.

ComparisonUndo in an ERP that overwrites valuesEvent sourcing + sagas
MethodOverwrite with earlier valuesAdd cancellation records with an opposite effect
Past contextFind the original conditions in separate history recordsKeep the original action, failure, and cancellation together
Impact on other workRestoring old values may lose changes made in betweenCheck later work and offset only the target task's effects
Audit and traceabilityWithout separate records, cancellation is hard to explainThe ledger keeps what happened and why it was cancelled

  5. Why use both together?

“Could we just add sagas to an existing ERP?” Yes. Sagas were originally designed for ordinary databases. But reliable undo needs two things.

  • Know the original values. If 100 units were reserved at KRW 1,250 after exchange rates and discounts, cancellation needs those terms and quantities, not today's price. The business records, called events, hold those values. With only current values, you must find them in separate history records.
  • Stay safe when a request arrives twice. Repeating the same request must give the same result (idempotency). If a response is lost and cancellation is requested twice, cancelling 100 units could turn into cancelling 200. Event versions, saga IDs, and request IDs filter out the second request. Event sourcing does not do this automatically; these checks must be built too.

A fixed workflow has steps and cancellation rules designed in advance. Agents choose tools as circumstances change. Even with cancellation rules for each tool, the system must read the completed work's records to decide what to undo. An ERP that overwrites values needs to collect those records separately, effectively creating another ledger.

  6. Test outcomes first with the same ledger

Because nothing was erased, past records are still available. Inventory records can show whether weak dehumidifier sales last summer came from low demand or stockouts. Sales totals alone cannot tell you that.

What-if analysis calculates the original plan and a changed plan side by side. It adds nothing to the ledger. The numbers come from the same models used on the ERP screen, not from the LLM. People can ask and agents can answer. Agents do not yet run these calculations and decide on their own.

Across 60 virtual companies, the median quarterly revenue error was 6.6%. These were virtual tests; results at real companies may be worse. The predicted ranges for a one-year outlook were too narrow, so the system warns when looking more than 180 days ahead.

Test first, act, and undo mistakes. All three use the same ledger.

  7. We tested it

We connected our own event-sourced uEngine ERP to the Process GPT Lite agent through MCP and ran it with a virtual company, Gaon Appliances.

Upload a tax invoice, then cancel in chat

Upload a tax invoice, then cancel in chat (Korean UI and narration)

  1. Upload, and it asks. An accounting employee uploads a tax invoice file to chat. Lite reads the supplier and amount (KRW 1,100,000), then asks, “Shall I process this in ERP?”

Reads the supplier and amount, then asks whether to proceed (Korean UI)

  1. Accept, and it processes the invoice. It finds the supplier and product, posts the voucher, and makes the payment. Inventory, accounting, and tax modules read those records and create the goods receipt, journal entries, and VAT totals. One saga ID ties the whole task together.
  1. Cancel in chat. Say, “I'll cancel what we just did.” We do not leave cancellation to the LLM. The server uses the ID to read the scope and check whether cancellation is allowed. It cancels the payment and posts a credit note in reverse order of the original actions. The relevant modules create stock returns and reversing journal entries. It also tells the user to request a corrected tax invoice from the supplier. Cancelling inside ERP does not correct external tax documents.

The server checks, then cancels the payment and voucher

  1. Check the numbers in the ledger. Both the original work and its cancellation remain. No records are erased.

Just ask in chat

Ask in chat and calculate without changing the ledger (Korean UI and narration)

This time, we use another virtual company with three years of records. Just ask a question in the usual chat window.

  • “What will sell the most in each quarter next year?” It gives the top product and its probability for each quarter. Air circulators have a probability of over 99% of ranking first in Q3 2027. Humidifiers have only a 39% probability in Q4. When it does not know, it says so.

Gives each quarter's top product and its probability

  • “Can I trust this forecast?” It runs the same calculation using the ledger from a year earlier and compares it with actual results. Revenue in all 3 completed quarters fell within the predicted ranges. It got the top product right in 2 quarters.
  • “What happens if we increase that product's order quantity to 1.5 times the current amount?” It estimates about KRW 46.64 million more revenue over the next year. Inventory value also rises by about KRW 7.72 million.

Calculates revenue and inventory changes from larger orders

  • “What if we raise the price by 10%?” The records do not show how customers would react, so it does not predict a direction. It only gives the break-even point: sales volume could fall by 19.4% with profit unchanged.

This conversation added 0 new records to the ledger. All figures come from a virtual company.

What we verified

Using a real LLM and real MCP, we ran voucher processing, cancellation in chat, and question-and-answer conversations 5 times each. All passed. These were local tests with virtual companies, not production results, and the Process GPT Lite integration will be released soon. Tax screens await expert review, and the app does not directly issue electronic tax invoices or submit electronic tax filings. Installation on real Supabase cloud has not yet been tested. A person must check the supplier and amount read from the file on the card.

  8. Success depends on a system that can handle mistakes

It is easy to focus only on the latest LLM and prompts. Once an agent operates ERP, the key question changes: “Can our system safely handle AI's mistakes?”

Not every system needs event sourcing. Microsoft's Event Sourcing pattern document also explains that traditional methods are enough for most systems. Start with systems where agents change transactions, inventory, and payments. Ask four questions before adopting them.

  • Can we ask what will change before acting?
  • Can we explain what the agent did?
  • Can we undo it without disturbing someone else's work?
  • If undo is not possible, can it stop and hand over to the person responsible?

Success depends on a system that can handle mistakes, not the model's intelligence.
